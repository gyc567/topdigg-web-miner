# Anthropic Mathematician Says Claude Completed a Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis; Stanford Releases an All-Human Competing Draft Hours Later

> Today's main thread is not the mathematics itself, it is who gets to certify it. On the same day, the Anthropic side announced a Riemann Hypothesis proof made with Claude, and the Stanford side released a competing draft written entirely by humans hours later. This is not one side embarrassing the other. It is the moment the industry has to define whether an AI-assisted proof counts as a proof. The takeaway for an operator is not the theorem, it is the prototype of the acceptance rule: when technical output first requires third-party endorsement, the industry will decide what standard it gets held to.
> The second thread is more alarming: the supply chain is leaking. The Together AI prompt disclosure, the two million canary link experiment, and the report that Cursor may drop Chinese open-weight models all say the same thing. The layer you assumed was secure is turning out not to exist at all.

## [Product Releases and Updates]

### 1. Qwen4 Max Lands on Together AI, Fixing a Whole Tornado Repo Bug in a Single Call
Together AI announced that Alibaba's flagship model Qwen4 Max is live on its serverless inference platform under the identifier Qwen/Qwen4-Max, with 1M token context and a 30 percent discount through November 22, 2026.

Source: [Together AI research and product blog](https://www.together.ai/blog/qwen4-max)

What is worth reading is the single-call fix of an entire Tornado repo bug, not the discount. A flagship model arriving on an inference platform is routine, but a vendor willing to demo one call clearing out defects across a whole repository shows agents moving from writing code to maintaining codebases. The decision to make now: when buying models, the evaluation criterion should shift from output quality on a single generation to how many defects one task can close out. The first measures leaderboards, the second measures real delivery cost.

## [Industry News]

### 1. Together AI Discloses a Prompt Leakage Incident Across Serverless Inference Services in June and July 2026
Together AI disclosed a security incident: an AI coding agent committed credentials for a private Hugging Face repository into a public GitHub repository called xorl, allowing an unauthorized party to download stored prompt samples submitted to serverless inference services including MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, and Kimi K2.6 in June and July 2026, and to search for credentials belonging to other services.

Source: [Together AI research and product blog](https://www.together.ai/blog/security-event-prompt-leakage-for-requests-in-june-and-july-of-2026)

This and the canary experiment further down are two halves of the same thing: if an agent has permission to write to a code repository, it also has permission to write your keys into a public repository. If your team runs agents inside a development workflow, or ships private deployments, the task today is to treat which repositories an agent may touch as a formal security review, not as a permissions detail. The leaked prompt samples are only the opening move. Credentials are what actually kills you.

## [Research and Papers]

### 1. Anthropic Mathematician Says Claude Completed a Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis; Stanford Releases an All-Human Competing Draft Hours Later
Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced on Friday that he produced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and the generalized Riemann Hypothesis together with Claude. The proof was released as 14 Lean data packages that would print to more than 250,000 pages, and Stanford released a competing draft written entirely by humans hours later.

Source: [The Decoder: AI News (RSS)](https://the-decoder.com/hours-after-anthropic-claims-a-claude-proof-of-the-riemann-hypothesis-stanford-releases-an-all-human-competing-draft/)

The two stories landing next to each other is the real signal: the industry is now arguing about who certifies AI output. Mathematics happens to be the one field where a machine can settle the answer, so it became the proving ground first. The standard an operator should carry away is simple. For anything AI helped produce, who signs off, who reviews, and who carries the liability. Once that rule is written down, it will be copied verbatim into every industry where AI touches delivery.

### 2. Researchers Planted Two Million Canaries in the AI Supply Chain and Uncovered Sensitive Data Leakage
Researchers planted two million canary links in requests routed through AI providers such as OpenRouter to test whether sensitive information leaks along the AI service chain. The results showed the data leaking out in large volume, like water through a sieve.

Source: [Ars Technica: AI (RSS)](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/11/a-leaky-ai-supply-chain-is-exposing-secret-user-data-at-an-alarming-rate/)

Two million probes is not a small experiment, which makes the conclusion hard to dismiss: as long as data passes through multiple routing layers, any one of them may keep a copy. If you buy models from third-party APIs, treat this as a cost line to be recomputed. More vendors means harder auditing, and the security budget has to grow with it.

### 3. Columbia and MIT Paper Shows Claude-Discovered Algorithms Break Long-Standing Speed Limits for 3SUM and APSP
A Columbia and MIT paper shows algorithms discovered by Claude achieving polynomial improvements on 3SUM and all-pairs shortest paths (APSP), reaching O(n^1.9992) for 3SUM and O(n^2.9995) for APSP, overturning the 3SUM and APSP conjectures.

Source: [X: Rohan Paul (@rohanpaul_ai)](https://x.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2124213113583174076)

Overturning the conjectures is what gives this weight. These two problems have been benchmark-hard for decades, not because nobody wanted to optimize them but because nobody had the idea. What teams in technical services should track is that the ordering of hard problems may have just been reshuffled, which means a list of tasks long considered impossible becomes quotable work within two years.

### 4. OpenAI Releases MedChemBench to Evaluate AI Models on Reading Molecular Structures and Experimental Data
OpenAI introduced MedChemBench, using 99 questions drawn from published research to evaluate how well AI models read molecular structures, interpret experimental evidence, and predict compound properties. Under the xhigh reasoning setting, GPT-6.2 Astra scored 84.5 percent usable, GPT-6 Astra 83.3 percent, GPT-6.1 Sol 82.0 percent, and GPT-5.6 Sol 58.2 percent.

Source: [OpenAI: official news (RSS)](https://openai.com/index/introducing-medchembench)

The gap between scores says more than the ranking. The previous generation trails by more than twenty points, meaning capability iteration is still on a steep slope. The phrase usable score also signals that many items are unscorable without penalizing the model. For operators in vertical AI, the lesson is this. A 99-question benchmark is not enough to pick a vendor, but it is exactly enough to judge how far a given field still is from genuinely usable.
