## 🏛 Central Banks and Policy

🦅 **Federal Reserve**: The October 28 FOMC voted 10:2 to leave the federal funds rate target range unchanged, chaired by new Chair Kevin Warsh, who stressed that rate decisions are unrelated to the midterm elections. Market interpretation of the Warsh path is sharply split: one camp sees him laying the groundwork for rate cuts via the formation of five working groups, while another camp (the consensus view at the November 2 market roundtable) sees a live possibility of a hike this year. The next meeting is December 9.

**Commentary**: This is the anchor for global asset pricing, and the anchor itself now has cracks. The extreme moves of the past two days are largely the product of one combination: "the AI capex boom needs low rates, and Warsh may not deliver." November nonfarm payrolls and December CPI are the two tests: if inflation rebounds on higher oil prices and tariffs, rate-cut expectations get pushed back, and AI valuations and interest rates get repriced at the same time — that's a second leg down, not the last one.

🏰 **ECB**: The October 30 ECB monetary policy meeting left all three key rates unchanged (policy rate 2.5%). With inflation at historic highs, the ECB faces further pressure to hike, and institutions such as ING now treat a December hike as the main support for the euro. Multiple media outlets have also reported that Lagarde has announced her early resignation as ECB President, though the news still awaits official confirmation.

**Commentary**: What the ECB is doing now is "watching from a high level + keeping the door open for the next hike." For anyone doing European business, that matters more than the numbers themselves: at EUR/USD 1.1226, European importers' costs are rising while European exporters are earning. If December brings a real hike, the pricing window for European markets tightens further, and you'll need to move faster than your competitors.

🇨🇳 **PBoC**: From November 2, the main policy rate officially "shifts its anchor," entering a "dual-anchor reform," with the loan pricing mechanism adjusted accordingly and the pricing benchmarks for mortgages and consumer loans changing in step. The LPR itself has been unchanged for 16 consecutive months. Meanwhile the State Council report explicitly supports Central Huijin-style stabilization funds, pointing to long-term capital entering A-shares.

**Commentary**: This is the policy that deserves the most careful reading today. It's not an RRR cut or a rate cut — it's a policy rate anchor shift in the pricing mechanism. Most peers will instinctively go looking for "was there an RRR cut," but the real impact is this: the interest-rate transmission chain has changed, so your cost of liabilities, your project funding math, and even your M&A valuation models all have to be redone. And the "Central Huijin-style stabilization fund" adds an institutional backstop for long-term money in A-shares — which November 5's A-share action already confirmed: the Shanghai Composite at 3689.91 (+0.10%), the Shenzhen Component at 11471.09 (+1.19%), the ChiNext Index at 2566.80 (+0.87%), and the STAR 50 at 1286.68 (+1.68%), closing higher against a 5.5% US selloff.

## 🌐 Diplomacy and Global Events

**US-China summit diplomacy**: The Democrats took both chambers of Congress in the US midterm elections. Foreign media analysts see Trump's room to use "pressure for concessions" in China talks compressed, and the pace of marginal change in tariff policy likely slowing in the short term; but another camp (Korean and US media) warns that a Democratic-controlled Congress will apply greater pressure on Taiwan and semiconductors, and that the legislative autonomy behind export controls will be strengthened.

**Commentary**: Two judgments need to be separated. In the short term, the China-US "uncertainty discount" falls, which is a genuine positive for anyone doing cross-border business — you no longer have to budget a hedge against whatever one White House individual tweets on any given day. In the long term, greater congressional autonomy means you can bet on specific provisions rather than on one person's stance — for peers playing industrial-policy arbitrage, that's a far more actionable information source. And the real risk in the "super AI ban" proposal is this: for the first time it inserts a regulatory variable into AI's valuation model, and once that variable goes from 0 to 1, it's the entire chain that gets repriced.

**China-EU trade**: China and the EU completed a new round of trade talks in Beijing in October. China agreed to cap exports of ordinary hybrid vehicles to the EU, simplify rare-earth export licensing, and cut tariffs on EU goods by €225 million, while the EU won market access for roughly €4 billion of goods. On November 5 the EU side still made "pressure for concessions" statements, but Lagarde herself spoke positively on EU-China cooperation. At the same time, China's leading power battery makers saw overseas installations surge 214.6% in the first three quarters, and several European countries (such as Portugal) are promoting EV adoption through subsidies.

**Commentary**: Simplified rare-earth licensing plus a halving of hybrid exports is real, tangible loosening for the industrial chain. But the model of "trading export controls for market access" has already taken shape, and the next round of talks will come quickly — anyone doing European business needs to learn to read the rhythm of negotiations rather than wait for the outcome. The more valuable line is batteries: overseas installations of +214.6% show that going overseas has shifted from selling whole vehicles to selling industrial chains, and entering at this point is far more comfortable than exporting vehicles.

**Geopolitics**: The Strait of Hormuz has still not reopened. Iran insists it will not reopen the strait until the US implements seven conditions (covering sanctions relief, war reparations, strait sovereignty and others), and the US military has struck Qeshm Island, with military friction on both sides continuing. The latest development: Iranian officials say Iran has delivered a nuclear deal proposal to the United States through Qatar; other reports say the US plans to approve Iranian oil sales from November 19, with the relevant countries providing fiscal support.

**Commentary**: Gold at $4,100 and today's decline in every risk asset both trace back to this. Iran's delivery of a nuclear deal proposal via Qatar is substantive progress, but "delivering a proposal" is still a long way from "reopening the strait," and every one of those seven conditions is a hard bone to crack. One practical suggestion for companies with shipping, energy or import-cost exposure: don't bet on the deal landing — put a Hormuz premium clause into your contracts.
