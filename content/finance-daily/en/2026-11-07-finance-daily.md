## 🏛 Central Banks and Policy

🦅 **Federal Reserve**: The October FOMC left the federal funds rate unchanged at 3.75%-4.00%, the second consecutive hold following a 25bp hike in September, the first increase since 2023, and two officials voted to hike 25bp. The statement says economic activity is expanding at a strong pace, domestic spending has been resilient, job gains have kept pace with the workforce and the unemployment rate has changed little, but inflation remains elevated and geopolitical developments leave uncertainty unusually high. Chair Warsh also announced that the Fed will reduce the number of policy meetings at which members vote on rates starting in 2027. Next meeting: December 9.

Comment: This is the anchor for global asset pricing, and the anchor itself now has cracks. A September hike, an October hold, and two members wanting another one, that combination tells you the Fed's tolerance for inflation is lower than the market assumes. The detail the market is ignoring is the cut in vote counts: a slower decision cadence from 2027 means every single speech gets priced earlier and more aggressively, so volatility rises structurally. For anyone doing cross-border flows or funding, the direct conclusion is to move hedging windows earlier rather than locking on the day of the decision.

🏰 **European Central Bank**: The October ECB monetary policy meeting left all three key rates unchanged, main refinancing at 2.65%, deposit facility at 2.50% and marginal lending at 2.90%, in line with expectations. The ECB warned that elevated energy prices could fuel inflation while weighing on activity, particularly if indirect and second-round effects intensify, though underlying inflation remains broadly stable with no significant wage response so far. At the post-meeting press conference Lagarde confirmed that the Governing Council did discuss a hike before deciding unanimously to hold. Multiple media reports say Lagarde has announced she will step down early as ECB President.

Comment: What the ECB did was discuss a hike and choose not to act, which leaves suspense for the market rather than direction. Combined with Lagarde's early departure, an undecided rate plus a departing President is the most fragile combination in European asset pricing. For anyone doing the European market: EUR/USD at 1.1140 helps exporters, but the twin uncertainties of the December rate path and the leadership vacuum will cap valuation expansion. Your quoting speed needs to beat the competition by half a step. Do not bet on a single variable.

🇨🇳 **People's Bank of China**: The October LPR was left at record lows for the 17th consecutive month, with the one-year rate at 3.0% and the five-year-and-above rate at 3.5%. Third-quarter GDP grew 4.5% year on year, beating expectations and reducing the urgency of stepping up support to hit the annual target. Governor Pan Gongsheng said the central bank will advance reforms to pricing benchmarks, shifting lending away from a relatively narrow reliance on the LPR toward a more diversified set of benchmarks. Analysts say the PBOC is considering switching its main policy rate to the 7-day overnight reverse repo rate to improve monetary policy transmission.

Comment: This matters far more than the LPR numbers themselves. Seventeen straight months of no change, plus an explicit re-anchoring, plus a possible switch of the policy rate to the reverse repo, is a complete package: de-emphasising the LPR as the anchor and letting the 7-day reverse repo become the true policy rate. That means the next thing to watch in rate liberalisation is not the monthly LPR quote but the volatility of open market operation rates. For anyone funding in RMB or running cross-basis arbitrage, the direct impact is that money market rates will move more than they have over the past two years. Rebuild the leverage cost model.

## 🌐 Diplomacy and Major Events

**US-China**: The US midterm results are in, with the Democrats taking both the House and the Senate in a blue sweep, leaving Trump a lame duck for the remainder of his term. Analysts are debating two consequences for China policy, greater pressure on Beijing on AI, and a clearer framework for bilateral talks. Meanwhile, Chinese and US defence officials have agreed to improve communication mechanisms to reduce risks, so official-level contact has not been severed. The US has also sanctioned Chinese companies over alleged military supplies to Cuba.

Comment: The real impact of this midterm on China is not tariffs, it is AI. A Democratic Congress turns something like the \"super AI ban\" from rhetoric into something with a legislative path, while 600,000 Nvidia chips sit stuck in Chinese customs. Two valves tightening simultaneously, technology restrictions and commercial clearance, is what explains why the AI supply chain has been selling off on valuation rather than on demand. For anyone in the AI business, the two things to track over the next two quarters are not leader summits but the pace of US congressional AI regulation and the actual export control list from the Commerce Department. Those two lists set your cost table, not any joint statement.

**China-Europe**: The US-EU China forum has come back to life after stalling since Trump's return, and the revival of the channel is itself a signal. Europe welcomed the Democratic landslide but worries over Ukraine, trade disputes and defence spending muted the optimism, and the EU's relationship with Washington has not loosened merely because the US changed parties. Meanwhile, the US and Brazil delivered a double blow to the populist right across Europe.

Comment: Europe is in a state of loosening in one direction while going nowhere in another. For anyone doing European business, a US pivot toward China does not mean Europe relaxes toward China. Quite the opposite: as Washington frees up its hands, Europe will carry more of the negotiating pressure from China, in the form of capacity cuts, anti-dumping and subsidy investigations. The barrier in the EU market over the next two years is not the tariff number, it is compliance and localisation. While the tariff window is still open, getting the product mix and compliance documentation right is worth more than chasing a small price advantage.

**Geopolitics**: Crude was choppy around $90 on Friday, briefly clawing back earlier losses, as investors weighed the risk of renewed US-Iran military escalation against the prospect of a diplomatic breakthrough. Iran reported attacks on oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, and UKMTO data indicates vessel attacks are continuing at a near-record rate. The same day, Iran reportedly sent a nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatar. The sticking points are clear: the US demands that Iran give up its nuclear capabilities, while Iran demands an end to the US naval blockade, sanctions relief and the release of frozen assets before it will fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Trump is reported to have been left with \"no good options\" on ending the Iran war following the midterm defeat.

Comment: This is the single largest variable in current asset pricing. The key to assessing the Iranian proposal is whether the exchange is balanced: Iran wants sanctions relief in return for reopening the passage, and the US is unlikely to accept that trade soon, because it amounts to admitting that Hormuz is a bargaining chip. So a \"diplomatic breakthrough\" is currently an option in the price, not the base case. The direct conclusion for owners: across oil, gold and shipping insurance, do not build budgets on a ceasefire as the base scenario. Use continued stalemate without escalation as the base case and full blockade as the stress test. That model is steadier than taking a directional bet.
