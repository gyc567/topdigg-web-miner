Markets were closed over the weekend, so all quotes below are reference closing prices from Friday, November 13. The single most important thing to understand today: at Friday's close, the market was trading the logic of "geopolitical de-escalation plus an AI pullback." But three moves over the weekend flipped that logic off the table.

First, in the early hours of November 14, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy of Iran announced that two tankers exploded while attempting to transit the Strait of Hormuz via an unauthorized route with radar and positioning systems switched off. Note the timing: Brent closed Friday at $95.84, essentially unmoved — none of this weekend news has been priced in.

Second, Nvidia still has no permanent CEO. On November 12, Jensen Huang resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, and the official announcement gave no reason; CFO Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO. As of November 13 the company responded that it had "not formally approached any potential candidates," yet the quarterly earnings report is due after the close on November 17.

Third, Trump flies to Shenzhen on the 18th, with the APEC Economic Leaders' Informal Meeting held November 18-19; on November 10 China extended the suspension of the 24% additional tariffs on US goods for another two months.

Forty-eight hours, three mutually independent event lines, all landing in the same week. For anyone allocating on equity, this is a risk window; for anyone running an industrial business chasing orders, this is a quoting window. Don't conflate the two.

The core of the disagreement isn't valuation. The position-sizing advice is unambiguous: don't use a heavy position at RSI 24.7 to bet on a bounce. Use a small position to wait for the 17th night to land — if you're wrong, the loss is contained; if you're right, add then. Beta of 2.25 means that if you size up heavily and you're wrong, you'll want to flip the table.

Freight, war-risk insurance and delivery lead times all three need to go into the quote. Conversely, for bosses running energy-related businesses, this week is a repricing window. ## 🏛 Central Banks & Policy

🦅 **The Fed**: On October 28, the FOMC held the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00%, staying on hold for a second straight meeting after the September 16 hike of 25 basis points (the first hike since July 2023). The statement said inflation remains elevated, domestic spending is holding up, and productivity and capital investment are strong, but geopolitical uncertainty is still high. Two members of the committee voted in favor of a 25 basis point hike. Chair Kevin Warsh succeeded Powell; at his post-meeting press conference he said that since the September hike, financial and credit conditions have tightened for some businesses and households, but overall financial conditions have not become tight, and the midterms coming up were not mentioned at the meeting. The meeting also announced procedural reforms: regular policy meetings will be reduced to 7 in 2027 and further to 6 in 2028. The next FOMC is December 8-9, the last one of the year.

Take: Two members calling for a hike means December rate-cut expectations can basically be cleared out, but there's also no sufficient evidence for a hike. So the marginal direction of global liquidity over the next two quarters is most likely flat or slightly tighter. For anyone holding leverage, holding compute, or holding long-duration assets, the cash cost over the next two quarters matters far more than the valuation story.

Another underappreciated signal is the reduction in the number of meetings. Slower policy reaction means bigger volatility — don't count on the Fed to smooth over every shock for you. The more practically useful conclusion for the boss: December 8-9 is the last policy window of the year, so don't bet year-end funding arrangements on a rate cut.

🏰 **The ECB**: The October meeting left all three key rates unchanged, with the main refinancing rate at 2.65% and the deposit facility rate at 2.50%, after the ECB chose to slow down in October following two hikes in June and September. Lagarde confirmed at the post-meeting press conference that the Governing Council had discussed a hike but unanimously agreed to hold, on the grounds that underlying inflation is broadly stable and wages have not yet reacted noticeably, while warning that persistently high energy prices could push inflation up. There are also reports that Lagarde plans to leave early.

Take: Europe's problem has never been policy — it's growth and energy costs. Lagarde's early departure on top of French bond volatility deserves more attention than the rate decision itself: during a leadership vacuum at the ECB, the market's pricing of the euro becomes more emotional. EUR/USD sliding near a 52-week low is exactly that reaction.

The call for the boss is direct: Europe is not your incremental market right now, at least not anymore. To judge whether a market is worth entering, look at three things: demand growth, barriers to entry, and whether you have an irreplaceable position. Europe has none of the three. Your incremental growth is in Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Latin America, and the demand being displaced at home.

🇨🇳 **The PBoC**: The October LPR was held steady for the 17th consecutive month, with the 1-year rate at 3.0% and the 5-year-plus rate at 3.5%, both at historic lows. Q3 GDP grew 4.5%, better than expected. Governor Pan Gongsheng said the central bank would advance reform of the pricing benchmark, reducing excessive reliance on a single LPR rate; the market accordingly judges that the PBoC may shift its policy rate anchor toward the 7-day reverse repo rate. The next LPR fixing is November 20.

Take: Better-than-expected data gives policy a reason not to hurry — but "better than expected" is itself a trap, because it keeps lowering the urgency of additional stimulus and thereby caps cyclical elasticity.

What genuinely deserves attention is the pricing-benchmark reform: LPR fading out and the 7-day reverse repo rate moving to the top means the interest-rate transmission chain is being rearranged. If you use LPR to model loan pricing, to quote installment plans to customers, or to write the cost of leverage into your quotes, you need to re-run your funding cost model after November 20. The window is unambiguous: five days from now.

## 🌐 Diplomacy & Major Events

**China-US Leaders & APEC**: Xi Jinping made a state visit to the United States from September 23 to 25, and the two leaders announced at the time plans to meet again in November and December. Trump confirmed he will attend the APEC Economic Leaders' Informal Meeting in Shenzhen on November 18-19, with the APEC Business Leaders' Summit on November 17-18 — his second visit to China within the same year. On the trade and economic front, on November 9 the State Council Tariff Commission, the Ministry of Commerce and the Ministry of Transport all issued notices on the same day, continuing the suspension of the 24% additional tariff rate on US goods for two months from 13:01 on November 10, while retaining the 10%. The US side's public agenda for this trip centers on agricultural purchases and energy. The Nvidia story keeps escalating, with the US Senate Banking Committee requesting that Nvidia and the Secretary of Commerce appear for a hearing.

Take: Lay out the timeline first. November 17, Nvidia reports after the close; November 17-18, the APEC Business Leaders' Summit; November 18-19, the APEC Economic Leaders' Informal Meeting, with Trump landing in Shenzhen. The heaviest earnings report in the AI industry and the heaviest trade negotiation between China and the US are less than 36 hours apart.

Whether this is deliberate scheduling or pure coincidence, the outcome is the same: the two will explain each other. If Nvidia's capital expenditure guidance on the 17th comes in weak, the US side loses one card in its hand at the negotiating table on the 18th; if the guidance is strong, the window for domestic substitution keeps narrowing.

The judgment for bosses in chips, servers, compute, rare-earth magnets and rare-earth land: this isn't sentiment, it's a repricing of the order book. China has already shown goodwill with the "two-month suspension of the 24% tariff" card, on condition that the overall direction stays stable during APEC. But watch two risks: first, don't bet on it being extended beyond early January; second, the export-control theme has already been through one round of trading. If the 17th earnings look bad, don't put your hope in policy — put your hope in your own delivery capability.

**China-EU Trade**: The EU announced it is abolishing the definitive anti-dumping and countervailing duties on solar glass originating from China — a combined dual rate of 84.2% — with the measure effective from October 22, 2026, and the duties already collected during the review period to be refunded without interest, with dual duties paid from July 22, 2025 eligible for refund applications. But the EU's overall direction on trade remedies against China has not turned: for three consecutive years it has strengthened anti-dumping and countervailing measures on steel, aluminum extrusions, ceramics, flooring and electric vehicles, and more exports are being routed through third countries for transshipment (Southeast Asia, Turkey, the Middle East). At the same time, Thailand has imposed an additional 30% tariff on Chinese e-commerce sellers, BMW's Shenyang joint venture announced large-scale layoffs amid fierce competition from domestic automakers, and a Dutch court ordered the destruction of seized documents in the BYD versus Jaguar Land Rover intellectual property case.

Take: The abolition of the 84.2% dual duties on solar glass with refunds is a genuine, hard benefit — but look clearly at its nature: this is a targeted pass under EU capacity and cost pressure, not a general pivot. In the same week that Thailand added 30% to Chinese cross-border e-commerce, which shows the pressure spilling over from the EU into Southeast Asia.

The real cost structure of Chinese manufacturing going overseas today is four layers stacked: tariffs + compliance barriers + local competition + IP litigation. If your overseas model still assumes "make it at home, sell it abroad, earn on low price," that model no longer holds after 2026. There are only two ways out: move the profit into localized production and local service, or build products that carry brand premium and are hard to route around.

**Geopolitics**: In the early hours of November 14, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy of Iran said two tankers exploded while attempting to sail through the Strait of Hormuz via a route not authorized by Iran, with radar and positioning systems switched off during the transit. Previously, Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi proposed compressing the "seven-day plan" into a "two-day plan" — on day one the United States takes a series of measures, on day two the Strait of Hormuz reopens and shipping returns to its prior state — conditional on the United States lifting the maritime blockade on Iranian ports and waiving sanctions on Iranian oil exports. On November 9 the Iranian side said the "two-day plan" remains valid, and that whether the strait opens depends on US political will and decisions. The IAEA has asked Iran to reopen its nuclear facilities for inspection.

Take: This is the item in this piece that bosses need to price into their cost tables immediately. Two tankers blew up in the strait, while the US side demands the blockade be lifted and the sanctions waived first — a textbook "who moves first" standoff.

Price has already given a signal once: Friday's Brent close of $95.84, essentially unchanged, with the market still betting on negotiation optimism. This weekend's explosion knocked a corner off that bet. An oil price gap-up at the open next week is a high-probability event, and freight, war-risk insurance and delivery lead times will follow. If you have orders in transit quoted at pre-war costs, that layer is now your counterparty. Conversely, for bosses running energy-related businesses, this week is a repricing window.
