Today's tape has exactly one storyline: **an eleven-day corporate governance drama ended in the most violent way possible.**

Nvidia's board voted unanimously to restore Jensen Huang as president, CEO and director, effective immediately, eleven days after he resigned. The stock opened up more than 30 percent and closed up 31.48 percent, adding over a trillion dollars in market value during the session and erasing essentially the entire decline since his departure. The Nasdaq jumped 6.51 percent, the Dow added 1,450 points, the S&P 500 rose 4.74 percent, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index surged 14.03 percent.

**But as someone who reads the tape every day, RSI at 42.9 matters far more to me than that 31 percent candle.**

**Commentary**: USD/JPY is holding around 159 while the offshore renminbi firmed slightly above 6.66. The real signal is the divergence between Europe and the United States. The euro fell 0.61 percent against the dollar in a single session, a distinctly one-directional move that has been rare in recent weeks. The cause is straightforward. The ECB held rates in October, but Lagarde announced her early departure and the central bank warned that stubbornly high energy prices could push inflation higher, so euro assets are being systematically trimmed. On the other side, the Fed's October minutes showed nearly every official believing further rate hikes are needed. One side of policy expectations tightens while the other loosens, and the euro naturally falls.

What this means for a business owner: if you have European orders or euro-denominated settlement exposure, expect the euro to stay weak over the next one to two quarters and thicken the currency buffer in your quotes. The flip side is that if a European customer pays you in euros, you are getting a better rate than you would have, so it is worth picking up the pace on collections.

## 📈 U.S. Equities

**Broad market**: Dow Jones +3.01% at 49,597.72, Nasdaq +6.51% at 24,089.56, S&P 500 +4.74% at 7,237.73. The Nasdaq and the S&P 500 both posted their best single-session performance since April 2025.

**🍎 Apple (AAPL)**: $349.08 (+1.90%), market cap $5.09 trillion, P/E 39.02 (forward P/E 37.17), analyst price target $345.63, RSI 52.6.

Apple was the most boring gain of the group, simply riding the index higher. But one detail deserves attention: **the consensus analyst price target of $345.63 now sits below the current price of $349.08**. In other words, sell-side analysts have not yet raised their targets, and the market has moved ahead of them. An average target across 45 analysts sitting below the spot price means the current level has moved outside the sell-side model. Either analysts will rush to revise upward, or the stock lacks a near-term catalyst for further re-rating. As a $5 trillion bellwether, a wave of upward target revisions from Apple typically triggers re-rating across an entire chain, so it is worth watching.

**🚗 Tesla (TSLA)**: $385.46 (+11.46%), market cap $1.53 trillion, P/E 425.52 (forward P/E 181.40), price target $417.53, RSI 52.1.

Tesla delivered the second-strongest gain of the session, and it came right after a 7.64 percent single-day drop on November 20, making this a textbook headline-driven rebound as Huang's return pulled the entire AI and tech risk appetite back. But the valuation argument still does not close. The static P/E is 425 times, and even on the forward number it is 181 times. As I wrote in a previous review, a story about full autonomous driving in 2030 cannot support a valuation multiple with zero cushion in an environment where rate hikes are being priced in. The multiple has not come down, and the rally has actually made the stock more expensive. The $417.53 target leaves only about 8 percent upside from spot, a safety margin about as thin as paper for a high-multiple name that just jumped 11 percent. The position case for Tesla now rests entirely on one assumption, whether you believe autonomous driving commercializes at scale in 2030. There is no second variable to catch you.

**🔥 NVIDIA (NVDA)**: $165.05 (+31.48%), market cap $3.99 trillion, P/E 17.73, price target $244.55, RSI 42.9.

**This is the single most important number in today's briefing.** The reaction cascaded through the chain: Micron +19.04%, SK Hynix +20.00%, KLA +14.96%, Applied Materials +14.62%, AMD +6.85%, and AI cloud provider CoreWeave up more than 22 percent.

**But look at RSI 42.9, because it matters far more than the percentage gain.** A genuine trend reversal should push RSI above 50 and keep it climbing. Right now it sits below 50, and the $165.05 price is simultaneously pressing against the 20-day moving average ($178.91) and the 50-day moving average ($206.08). Translated into plain language: **this is a repair rally off an oversold extreme, not a trend reversal.** NVDA closed at $125.53 on November 20, the lowest close in six months, and closed at $165.05 today, still 39 percent below the October high of $239.24.

So here is the call for a business owner: do not read today's 31 percent candle as evidence that the AI trade has restarted. The Nasdaq and the Dow just posted their largest weekly decline in more than six years, and the Nasdaq 100 came within touching distance of a bear market. That kind of wound is not healed by a single candle. This is better understood as an outsized technical rebound triggered by the clearing of a governance overhang. **If you want AI exposure, keep it, but size it to survive a second test of the lows rather than to chase strength.**

There is one more layer most people are missing. Dan Ives kept his outperform rating and $225 price target, while noting that the independent committee investigation is still ongoing, export control questions remain unresolved, and the full value of Huang once governance doubts are fully cleared sits somewhere between $225 and $300. In other words, **the largest upside comes from governance being settled, not from a stock that has already risen 31 percent.**

**Commentary**: During the U.S. session on November 23, December gold futures on the New York Mercantile Exchange rose more than 4 percent at one point and reclaimed $3,600 per ounce, while December silver futures climbed more than 5.5 percent above $43. That momentum carried over into today. Two forces combined: the tech rally pulled liquidity back into the risk complex, and safe-havens and risk assets rose together, which is unusual. On top of that, bargain buying and short covering moved at the same time. Silver gaining nearly twice as much as gold continues the earlier logic. This is an inflation hedge trade, not a flight to safety.

**The crude oil line is the most counterintuitive and most valuable call of the day.** At dawn today, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy announced that three tankers had struck mines and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz within the past few hours, one near the southern coast of the strait and two in the east, and that the vessels were under tight U.S. air cover and escort at the time. On the same day, Saudi airstrikes on a telecom facility in Hodeidah, Yemen, killed 17 people. **The strait is bleeding and the price of oil is falling.** Supply oversupply logic overwhelmed the geopolitical premium, and WTI closed below the $80 handle.

Here is the concrete conclusion for a business owner: **if your cost base tracks oil prices, whether in chemicals, logistics, plastics, textiles or shipping fuel, the next one to two quarters are a window for cost improvement. Do not build rising cost assumptions into your quotes.** But keep two things separate. Fuel costs are falling while the actual navigational risk in Hormuz is pushing insurance premiums higher. Three tankers striking mines while under U.S. escort means that risk premium keeps climbing, so anyone with shipping exposure should pull insurance quotes out into their own line item. And on the flip side, **if your business is not energy-linked, do not expect geopolitical conflict to turn into profit for you.** That transmission chain has been broken in 2026 by oversupply.

## 🏛 Central Banks and Policy

**🦅 The Federal Reserve**: federal funds rate 3.75 percent. The October FOMC minutes, released November 18, showed nearly all officials judging that further rate hikes would be needed in the baseline case to return inflation to the 2 percent target in a timely manner, with some noting that if upcoming inflation data proves unfriendly, the next meeting would need to consider a one-time increase of more than 25 basis points. Next meeting: December 8-9. Latest development: on November 23 Treasury Secretary Bessent warned that if the Fed tightens excessively in response to the latest inflation data it could endanger the U.S. economy, and pointed to signs of fragility in the financial system.

**Commentary**: Bessent's comment is the real policy signal of the day, more so than any set of minutes. He is talking about over-tightening risk and financial system fragility, while the October minutes are about nearly every official believing rate hikes are needed. **Central bank officials say they should hike, the Treasury secretary says they must not, and that divergence is itself the story for the December meeting.** Add the Thanksgiving holiday and the early Friday close, when liquidity is thin to begin with, and if you are planning 2027 financing, December 8-9 is a date you have to watch. Do not wait for the outcome before adjusting your rate plan.

**🏰 The European Central Bank**: the October meeting left rates unchanged, with the main refinancing rate at 2.65 percent and the deposit facility rate at 2.50 percent. The ECB warned that persistently high energy prices could push inflation higher and weigh on economic activity, particularly through indirect and second-round effects, while noting that underlying inflation was broadly stable and no significant wage response has appeared. Governor Lagarde announced plans to step down early, drawing attention amid heightened volatility in the French bond market. After the meeting Lagarde confirmed that the ECB had discussed a rate hike before agreeing unanimously to hold.

**Commentary**: This is the direct cause of today's euro weakness. The four words "discussed a hike" carry far more weight than "left unchanged", and combined with Lagarde's early departure the ECB sits in the most fragile combination for eurozone assets, a possible turn in policy alongside a leadership handover. If you do European market entry, European compliance or euro settlement work, uncertainty rises noticeably over the next two quarters, and today's euro move is already pricing that in.

**🇨🇳 The People's Bank of China**: on November 20 the LPR was announced at 3.00 percent for one year and 3.50 percent for terms over five years, unchanged for the eighteenth consecutive month and in line with expectations. This was the first quotation after the main policy rate was re-anchored. Since November 2 the central bank has conducted overnight reverse repos each working day at a fixed rate with quantity bidding, with the operating rate at 1.25 percent, while seven-day reverse repos shifted to fixed-quantity, rate-bidding operations. The binding constraint is bank net interest margins. Second-quarter net interest margin at commercial banks was 1.41 percent, up 1 basis point from the first quarter and the first sequential quarterly recovery since Q1 2022, yet the absolute level remains in the historical low range. The third-quarter monetary policy report reaffirmed "appropriately accommodative" policy, and the weighted average rate on new corporate loans in September fell to 2.96 percent, dropping below 3 percent for the first time.

**Commentary**: The re-anchoring is being widely misread. The operating rate moved from 1.40 percent to 1.25 percent, which looks like 15 basis points of easing, but the central bank was explicit. **This is not a rate cut, only the confirmation of an already-existing overnight funding rate as the policy rate, and it does not change banks' actual cost of funds.** What matters more is that this was the first quotation after the re-anchoring and the quoting banks did not cut their spreads, which mechanically widened the nominal spread by roughly 15 basis points on the new benchmark. Put plainly, **China's window for a rate cut is genuinely being pushed out**, and the market's view that it may slip to 2027 is well founded. For domestic companies, funding costs will not get cheaper in the near term, but they will not get more expensive either. The real question is when bank net interest margins will make room, and until that variable improves, loan rates will struggle to fall.

## 🌐 Diplomacy and Major Events

**U.S.-China relations and technology controls**: alongside Huang's reinstatement, Nvidia moved forward on governance restructuring. The board has formed a National Security and Export Control Committee composed entirely of independent directors, and has begun recruiting a Chief Compliance Officer responsible for overseeing export controls and other compliance matters. Trump wrote on Truth Social that the U.S. government holds no evidence of any illegal conduct and that there is no reason to believe Huang's return poses any national security risk. Treasury Secretary Bessent's language softened from calling for an external candidate to saying he is now already on the outside.

**Commentary**: This eleven-day circus was effectively the White House, the Treasury, Silicon Valley and Wall Street joining forces to keep one man in his seat. Elon Musk publicly signed the petition, and the "Stand with Jensen Huang" effort collected more than 200,000 signatures, while Altimeter Capital and Atreides Management jointly wrote to the board.

**But here is the point that has to be made clearly: export controls will not loosen because Huang is back.** Quite the opposite. Nvidia tightened its China export strategy during his absence, and on returning he is simultaneously establishing an export control committee and hiring a chief compliance officer. This is institutional tightening, not the restoration of one person's attitude. Huang's own past logic was that if Nvidia dominated the Chinese market China would struggle to develop its own high-end chips, and that over-tightening by the United States would push China toward alternatives and accelerate Chinese domestic substitution. That argument has demonstrable force after what happened during his absence.

**So the conclusion for Chinese companies is unambiguous: do not let Huang's return slow the pace of your own innovation.** Nvidia's chips are hard to replace but not impossible to replace. AMD and Intel compete on the U.S. side, while Cambricon, MetaX and Moore Threads have products that already earn recognition from domestic large-model companies for inference workloads. The real strengths of Chinese chipmakers are the engineer dividend, a complete manufacturing ecosystem and a vast home market. What they lack is ecosystem stickiness, and ecosystem stickiness is exactly what AI can help build, since constructing an ecosystem isomorphic to CUDA with AI tooling is already a path being proposed in the industry. **The most valuable thing to invest in along the chip supply chain is not compute procurement but software and adaptation services on top of domestic compute stacks.**

**China-Europe trade**: EU tariffs and countervailing duties on Chinese electric vehicles and related sectors remain the most concentrated friction point in the China-Europe relationship, and negotiations over alternatives such as minimum price undertakings remain deadlocked.

**Commentary**: Real-time search was limited for this section, so the above is compiled from recent public background and official announcements should be treated as authoritative. The direction, however, is unlikely to change. The China-Europe contest over electric vehicles will not wrap up quickly, and any supply chain dependent on the single EU market must keep a non-European option open. That is no longer contingency planning, it is a fact on the cost sheet. Add today's uncertainty at the ECB leadership level and a weakening euro, and price competitiveness for Chinese exports into Europe is deteriorating. Businesses selling into Europe need to prepare for currency and tariff pressure at the same time.

**Geopolitics**: in the early hours of November 24, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy announced that three tankers had struck mines and exploded in the Strait of Hormuz over the previous few hours, one near the southern coast of the strait and two in the east, while under tight U.S. air cover and escort. At the same time, media controlled by Houthi forces in Yemen reported, citing telecommunications authorities in Houthi-controlled areas, that an airstrike by Saudi aircraft on an under-construction communications facility in Hodeidah, a Red Sea port city in western Yemen, raised the death toll to 17 with 11 injured, destroying a communications tower and cutting telephone and internet service across several districts.

**Commentary**: Tankers striking mines while under U.S. escort is no longer something you can describe as a war risk premium. It means **the waterway itself is no longer reliable**. For anyone doing foreign trade, this is the most practically useful item today. War-risk insurance premiums, rerouting costs and schedule stability in Hormuz will all become hard constraints over the next one to two quarters rather than soft costs. **When quoting on Middle East orders, list insurance and rerouting as separate line items**, because if something goes wrong the arithmetic simply will not work otherwise.
