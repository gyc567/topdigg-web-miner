## 🏛 Central Banks and Policy

🦅 **The Fed**: On Oct 28 the FOMC held the federal funds target range unchanged on a 10-2 vote, chaired by new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh. Markets read the statement as hawkish on the surface but a runway-building exercise for cuts: holding rates to anchor inflation expectations while quietly forming five working groups to preserve room to ease. Most analysis still points to one 25bp cut in 2026. The next meeting is Dec 9.

**Takeaway**: This is the anchor for global asset pricing today. Warsh's "new chair, new toolkit" means the market has to relearn the Fed's reaction function, and that learning curve is itself a source of volatility. November payrolls and December CPI are the two tests for the cut path. The important tension is that AI capex boom versus high rates is now a genuine fault line inside the Fed. If AI spending can genuinely carry growth, the urgency to cut falls; if not, AI valuations and rates get repriced together. That is the single variable every AI-concentrated operator should be tracking.

🏰 **The ECB**: At the Oct 29 policy meeting the ECB announced it would hold rates, the first pause after two consecutive 25bp hikes in June and September. That is a shift into observation mode, not a turn toward easing.

**Takeaway**: Europe has moved from "catching up on easing" to "watching from a high plateau," and the read-across for Chinese exporters and euro-settled revenue matters more than the numbers themselves. At EUR/USD 1.124, European companies converting dollar revenue into euros are better off, while European importers face a higher cost base. If you run cross-border e-commerce or sell into Europe, the next two quarters hinge on whether the ECB turns hawkish again in December. That decision sets your European pricing window.

🇨🇳 **The PBOC**: The LPR has been unchanged for 16 consecutive months, with both the 1-year and 5-year-plus benchmarks steady. The policy rate has been flat since a 10bp cut in May 2025. Expectations of an RRR and rate cut this year persist (Goldman Sachs pushed its "double cut" call from Q4 2025 to Q1 2026), but the central bank's language remains anchored on "ample liquidity."

**Takeaway**: 16 flat months on the LPR, plus strong exports and a rebound in CPI and PPI, tell you the PBOC believes the fundamentals are resilient and does not need to ease quickly. The real change is in equities. On Nov 4 the Shanghai Composite rose 2.16% to 3,686.17, the ChiNext gained 6.4% to 2,544.73, and the STAR 50 jumped 9.92% to 1,265.47, with turnover back above 2 trillion yuan, more than 4,600 stocks higher and over 100 limit-ups, led by semiconductors and precious metals. This is not liquidity loosening. It is a repair in risk appetite, which is a fundamentally different animal.

## 🌐 Diplomacy and Global Events

**US-China**: The economic and trade agreement reached in Korea on Nov 1, 2025 continues to be implemented. The newest variable is the US midterms, where Democrats swept both chambers. Policy friction between a Democratic Congress and the sitting administration will now be far higher, giving Congress more independent legislative authority over tariffs and chip export controls. On election day the Dow briefly jumped 1,200 points with tech broadly leading, showing the market is already pricing a new administration more willing to spend on infrastructure and AI.

**Takeaway**: Read this in two layers. In the short run, a Democratic Congress likely slows the pace of marginal tariff changes, so the "uncertainty discount" on US-China trade compresses, which is a real positive for anyone doing cross-border business. Over the longer run, greater congressional autonomy means you can hedge specific provisions rather than one White House individual's rhetoric. For operators who trade on industrial policy, that is a far more actionable information source.

**China-EU**: Two days of trade talks in Beijing ended on Oct 9 with an understanding on export controls and rare earths. China agreed to cap shipments of ordinary hybrid vehicles to the EU and to cut tariffs on EU goods by EUR 225 million, while simplifying rare-earth export licensing. The EU secured market access for roughly EUR 4 billion in goods. Analysts judge the risk of a full-blown trade war has dropped sharply.

**Takeaway**: The most underrated item in today's brief. Simplified rare-earth licensing plus reduced hybrid exports is concrete industrial-chain loosening. Rare earths are the lifeblood of magnets, robotics and semiconductor packaging, so lighter licensing hands downstream manufacturers breathing room. At the same time, note that the EU's playbook of trading for China's export-control concessions is now established, and there will be another round. People doing Europe business need to learn to read the negotiation rhythm rather than wait for a verdict.

**Geopolitics**: The Strait of Hormuz remains the biggest risk in the world. Iran has made clear the strait will not fully reopen until the US meets its conditions. Qatar is actively pushing for renewed US-Iran talks, and the framework of a Hormuz shipping memorandum has tentatively taken shape, but both sides give conflicting accounts of the details. Several countries have begun planning pipelines that bypass the waterway.

**Takeaway**: That is the core explanation for gold at +3.9% and silver at +5.3%. A reopening of the strait is a sword of Damocles hanging over precious metals, and any concrete US-Iran deal would trigger a sharp drop. At these levels I would take partial profit first, then let the progress of negotiations decide whether to add. For anyone with shipping, energy or import-cost exposure, this is the variable worth watching daily.
